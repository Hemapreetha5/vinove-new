Guide to terminology

Each individual investor has their own attitude to risk and return and sets his/her investment objectives accordingly. The investor may then monitor whether these objectives are met on an ongoing basis. It is possible to calculate the return on his/her investments and then adjust that measure to take account of the risk that he/she has undertaken. The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) analysed the extent to which past performance is of use to consumers in making investment decisions. It was concerned that if using past performance, advisers should consider it in conjunction with other factors, including price and risk, when making investment decisions. The FCA is continuing to review industry practice and what measures can best meet consumers' needs.

ABI Sector

The aim of the Association of British Insurers (ABI) is that all the funds in one sector are broadly comparable, making it easier for investors to compare like with like when deciding which fund is most suitable for them.

The categories are not specifically designed to “risk grade” funds. They are designed to group together funds which are similar, so that they may be more easily compared on a like-for-like basis.

The Morningstar Rating™ is a quantitative assessment of a fund’s past performance -both return and risk- as measured from one to five stars. It uses focused comparison groups -Morningstar Categories- to better measure manager skill. The rating is also adjusted for all sales charges and exit fees to reflect the actual returns received by the investor. Within each category, Morningstar awards 10% of funds one star, 22.5% of funds two stars, 35% three stars, 22.5% four stars, and 10% receive five stars.

The Morningstar Rating™ is based on utility theory, which recognises that investors are more concerned about a possible poor outcome than an unexpectedly good outcome. The risk adjustment thus places greater emphasis on downward variations in returns. The rating rewards consistent performance and reduces the possibility of strong short-term performance masking the inherent risk of a fund. Each fund is rated over three-, five-, and ten-year periods depending on the length of its performance history. These ratings are then aggregated into the Overall Morningstar Rating™, which is the rating published here.

Further information on Morningstar, its measures and fund performance data is available online at www.morningstar.co.uk and Adviser Workstation.

FE fundinfo Crown Ratings

FE fundinfo Crown Fund Ratings range from one to five, and are designed to help investors identify funds which have displayed superior performance in terms of stock pricing, consistency and risk control.

FE fundinfo Crown Ratings Methodology

FE fundinfo Crown Fund Ratings are calculated by building up a score. FE fundinfo builds up a score based on analysing a fund's performance over the last three years. The score is made up of three components – alpha, relative volatility, and consistently good performance*. Each part is calculated against a benchmark for the fund.

(* See Risk Performance analysis section of this guide for further explanation of investment terms.)

Calculating the score and assigning the FE fundinfo Crown Fund Rating

Funds are grouped in ten sub-asset classes, and for each of the tests the raw scores are allocated from zero (worst) to 100 (best). Within each grouping, funds are given FE fundinfo Crowns based on their total scores, using the following distribution:

The top 10% of scores in each grouping – five FE fundinfo Crowns

The next 15% - four FE fundinfo Crowns

The next 25% - three FE fundinfo Crowns

The next 25% - two FE fundinfo Crowns

The bottom 25% - one FE fundinfo Crowns

Exclusions

Funds with short history are excluded for technical reasons. The following sectors are also excluded:

  • Unclassified
  • Personal Pensions
  • Protected Funds and Structured Products

Risk Performance Analysis

Volatility (absolute risk)

Volatility Risk is defined as the annualised standard deviation of the monthly returns of a fund over a three-year period. The standard deviation measures how far the actual fund returns have deviated from the fund's average return. The more a fund's returns have varied from the average, the higher the volatility. The volatility of a fund is partly driven by the asset types to which it is exposed. A fund exposed to equities will normally have a higher volatility than a fund with no equity exposure.

A fund with high absolute risk exhibits greater uncertainty of return than a fund with low absolute risk.

Beta (relative risk)

Beta is a risk measure of a fund's sensitivity to movements in the market (or benchmark). As a risk measure it is driven by a Fund Manager's style bias - a high Beta indicating a more aggressive stance whereas a low Beta indicates a more cautious stance. Beta is only really of use when calculated against a 'relevant' benchmark. If there is little correlation between the fund and the benchmark, then the resultant Beta will be of little use as a risk measure. Beta is retrospective, based on the historical position of a fund. It does not tell the investor anything about what the fund manager may do in the future.

By definition, the market/benchmark has a Beta of 1. A figure greater than 1 indicates that the fund will tend to outperform in a rising market and under underperform in a falling one, i.e. it is more volatile than the market. The reverse applies to a figure of less than 1.

Alpha

Alpha is a measure of the additional return that a fund (or fund manager) generates above the return expected from passively tracking the relevant asset class, sector or market. It takes the volatility of the fund and compares its risk-adjusted performance to the benchmark index. The excess return of the fund relative to the return of this benchmark index is known as the fund's alpha. A positive alpha of 1.0 means the fund has outperformance its benchmark by 1%, and conversely a negative alpha of 1.0 would mean an underperformance of 1.0%.

Jensen Alpha

While Beta is a risk measure of a fund's sensitivity to 'market' or benchmark movements, Jensen Alpha is the excess return over and above that resulting from its benchmark risk. In other words, Jensen Alpha is the difference between the return you would expect from a fund, given its Beta, and the return that it actually produced. If a fund returns more than its Beta would predict, it has a positive Jensen Alpha, and if it returns less than the amount predicted by Beta, the fund has a negative Jensen Alpha.

A fund's return and its risk contribute to its Jensen Alpha value.

In theory an investment manager exhibits a positive Jensen Alpha if he or she has superior stock picking or market timing skills, while a passive or index tracking fund would not generate any positive Jensen Alpha.

Knowing the Jensen Alpha of any two funds will allow you to tell which fund has performed better according to this risk-adjusted measure. Jensen Alpha will allow the investor to get a quick feel for a fund's risk-adjusted performance relative to the peer group. A negative Jensen Alpha indicates underperformance.

Jensen Alpha is often seen as a key measure of the 'added value' given by an active fund manager.

Risk Performance Analysis

Sharpe Ratio

The 'risk-free' rate of return is assumed to be cash held on deposit in a bank or building society where it is subject to no risk conditions. Reward for taking on risk can only be given above this risk-free rate of return.

The Sharpe Ratio is a widely used risk-adjusted return measure. It is defined as the Relative Return divided by Absolute Volatility. It demonstrates the relationship between a fund's risk and its return. The underlying assumption is that a fund manager could invest in a riskless asset, therefore the return of the risk-free asset is deducted from the annualised average. This net return is then divided by the total risk. The higher a Sharpe Ratio the better a portfolio's returns have been relative to the amount of absolute risk taken. A negative ratio indicates that the fund actually made less than the risk-free amount.

R2

Reflects how much of a fund's return is determined by the market/benchmark return. The maximum R2 is 1. This means that 100% of a fund's movement is determined by the benchmark. A number progressively lower than 1 shows that the fund's return becomes more independent.

InformationRatio

The Information Ratio is a measure of how successful the fund manager has been at taking risk relative to the market/benchmark. A fund that has taken a low level of risk and achieved a higher return than the market/benchmark will have a high and positive information ratio. A fund manager that has lagged the market/benchmark in performance terms will have a negative Information Ratio.
